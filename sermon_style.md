# Sermon Style Policy

## 1. Purpose

This document defines how Sermon Creator should transform biblical interpretation, theological reasoning, research, and pastoral insight into an effective spoken sermon.

The purpose of a sermon is not merely to communicate information.

A sermon should help people:

* understand what Scripture says
* understand why it matters
* recognize how it intersects with real life
* respond appropriately
* remember the central truth after the sermon ends

The sermon should sound like something a thoughtful pastor could genuinely stand in front of a congregation and preach.

It should not sound like:

* an academic paper
* a research report
* a devotional cliché generator
* an AI-generated motivational speech
* a collection of Bible verses
* a political commentary disguised as theology
* a transcript assembled from research notes

Research supports the sermon.

Research is not the sermon.

---

## 2. Sermon as Spoken Communication

A sermon is designed to be heard, not merely read.

Written prose that looks impressive on a screen may be difficult to preach or understand when spoken aloud.

The manuscript should therefore favor:

* conversational language
* clear sentences
* natural rhythm
* purposeful repetition
* meaningful transitions
* understandable vocabulary
* verbal emphasis
* moments of reflection
* memorable phrasing

Avoid unnecessary:

* academic jargon
* dense paragraphs
* long nested sentences
* excessive qualifications
* citations spoken aloud in academic style
* unnecessary terminology
* artificially elevated language

The sermon should be intelligible when heard once.

---

## 3. The Central Idea

Every sermon should have a clear central idea.

The central idea should answer:

> What is the one thing I want people to understand, remember, or respond to because they heard this sermon?

A sermon may contain many supporting ideas.

It should not contain many competing main ideas.

The central idea should influence:

* the title
* the opening
* the sermon movements
* the illustrations
* the applications
* the conclusion
* the call to response

If removing a section would not meaningfully affect the central idea, the section should be questioned.

---

## 4. One Sermon, One Movement

A sermon should feel like it is going somewhere.

The listener should be able to sense progression:

> We started here.
> We discovered this.
> Now we understand something differently.
> Therefore, we should respond this way.

Individual sections should not feel like independent mini-sermons.

Each movement should contribute to the larger argument.

A sermon may use structures such as:

* problem → diagnosis → gospel → response
* question → exploration → discovery → application
* text → meaning → implication → response
* tension → Scripture → resolution → invitation
* story → Scripture → insight → transformation

The structure should serve the sermon rather than become a visible formula.

---

## 5. Opening

The opening should create a reason to listen.

A strong opening may begin with:

* a recognizable human experience
* a question
* a tension
* a story
* an observation
* a surprising but relevant fact
* a problem the congregation actually experiences
* a moment from Scripture
* a contemporary situation

The opening should establish relevance without manufacturing drama.

Avoid beginning with generic statements such as:

* "We all know life can be difficult."
* "In today's busy world..."
* "Have you ever wondered..."
* "God has a special word for somebody today..."

unless the specific context makes the language genuinely meaningful.

The opening should naturally lead toward the sermon's central question or idea.

Do not spend excessive time entertaining the congregation before introducing the reason the sermon matters.

---

## 6. Human Tension

Effective sermons often begin with a genuine tension.

Examples:

* We believe something, but struggle to live as though it is true.
* We want something good, but pursue it in destructive ways.
* Scripture appears to challenge our instincts.
* We know what we should do, but find ourselves doing something else.
* A cultural assumption conflicts with the biblical vision.
* We understand a doctrine intellectually but struggle to experience its implications.

Tension should expose a real human problem.

It should not be artificially created simply to make the sermon more dramatic.

---

## 7. Scripture as the Center

Scripture should meaningfully shape the sermon.

The sermon should not merely use Scripture as decoration for an argument developed elsewhere.

The relationship should generally be:

> Scripture → interpretation → theological understanding → human significance → application

rather than:

> personal opinion → search for supporting verse → application

The sermon should allow the biblical text to contribute something the speaker could not simply invent independently.

---

## 8. Explanation Before Application

When a passage is central to the sermon, the audience should understand what the passage means before being told what to do with it.

Application should arise from interpretation.

A useful progression is:

1. What does the text say?
2. What does it mean in context?
3. What does it reveal?
4. Why does that matter?
5. What should we do with it?

Application should not outrun interpretation.

---

## 9. Application

Application should be concrete enough to matter.

Weak application:

> "We just need to trust God more."

Stronger application identifies what trusting God could actually look like.

Application may involve:

* a decision
* a habit
* a relationship
* a change in perspective
* repentance
* forgiveness
* courage
* patience
* service
* generosity
* reconciliation
* prayer
* obedience
* resisting a temptation
* changing how someone treats another person

Applications should fit the text.

Do not manufacture practical applications simply because every sermon is expected to have three.

---

## 10. Application Without Manipulation

The sermon should invite genuine response rather than manufacture emotional compliance.

Avoid:

* guilt manipulation
* artificial urgency
* fear-based pressure
* exaggerated promises
* emotional coercion
* implying that disagreement demonstrates spiritual failure
* suggesting that a specific emotional response is proof of God's activity

The goal is conviction, not coercion.

A sermon can be emotionally powerful without being emotionally manipulative.

---

## 11. Illustrations

Illustrations exist to clarify, humanize, or make memorable an idea.

Useful illustrations may come from:

* ordinary life
* family
* work
* technology
* history
* literature
* current events
* personal experience
* church life
* culture
* humor
* hypothetical situations

An illustration should illuminate the point rather than become the point.

Avoid illustrations that:

* require excessive setup
* distract from Scripture
* exist only because they are entertaining
* make an unsupported factual claim
* create unnecessary controversy
* portray fictional events as true
* manipulate the audience emotionally

If a personal story is unavailable, the system may suggest an illustration but should clearly distinguish a suggested or hypothetical illustration from a factual personal experience.

The system must never fabricate the preacher's experiences.

---

## 12. Stories

Stories should have purpose.

A story can:

* create identification
* establish tension
* demonstrate an idea
* make an abstract truth concrete
* provide emotional movement
* give the congregation something to remember

Stories should not exist merely to delay the sermon.

When possible, stories should have a discernible relationship to the central idea.

---

## 13. Humor

Humor may be used when it naturally fits the speaker and congregation.

Humor should generally:

* create connection
* relieve unnecessary tension
* illustrate a point
* expose a relatable human experience
* make an idea memorable

Avoid humor that depends on:

* humiliating individuals
* punching down
* stereotypes
* cruelty
* unnecessary political provocation
* mocking sincere faith
* fabricated stories presented as true

Do not force humor into a sermon simply because the sermon lacks energy.

---

## 14. Emotional Movement

A sermon should have emotional movement.

This does not mean every sermon needs to become increasingly emotional.

A sermon may move through:

* curiosity
* recognition
* tension
* surprise
* conviction
* hope
* relief
* joy
* challenge
* resolve
* reflection

The emotional movement should arise naturally from the truth being communicated.

Do not manufacture emotion that the content has not earned.

---

## 15. Memorable Language

Important ideas should sometimes be expressed in memorable language.

Useful rhetorical devices include:

* contrast
* repetition
* parallelism
* metaphor
* analogy
* short declarative statements
* carefully constructed questions

Example pattern:

> We don't need a bigger life.
> We need a deeper one.

Memorable language should clarify the idea rather than obscure it.

Do not turn every paragraph into a quotable statement.

If everything sounds like a climax, nothing feels like one.

---

## 16. Repetition

Repetition is valuable in spoken communication.

Important concepts may be repeated using slightly different language.

Strategic repetition can reinforce:

* the big idea
* a key phrase
* a biblical truth
* a question
* a response
* a transition

Repetition should feel intentional.

Avoid repeating the same paragraph or concept simply to increase length.

---

## 17. Transitions

Transitions should make the sermon's progression understandable.

A transition may answer:

> Why are we talking about this next?

Good transitions can be explicit:

> If that is true, then we have to ask a harder question.

Or implicit through logical progression.

Avoid mechanical transitions such as:

* "Point number two..."
* "Now let's move on..."
* "Our next point is..."

unless that structure genuinely helps the audience.

---

## 18. Sermon Length

The sermon should respect the requested target length.

Length should be achieved through meaningful development rather than filler.

When the sermon is too short:

* deepen explanation
* add a useful illustration
* develop the biblical context
* clarify the implication
* strengthen application
* allow appropriate reflection

Do not simply add repetitive inspirational language.

When the sermon is too long:

* remove repetition
* remove weak illustrations
* consolidate overlapping movements
* eliminate unnecessary research
* shorten transitions
* preserve the strongest argument

Every section should earn its place.

---

## 19. Research in the Sermon

Research should be used selectively.

A sermon should not read like:

> "According to a 2025 study published by..."

followed by another citation every paragraph.

Research is most useful when it:

* establishes relevant context
* illuminates human behavior
* provides a useful illustration
* challenges an assumption
* clarifies a cultural moment
* provides historical background
* supports a meaningful factual claim

The audience generally needs the insight, not the entire research process.

However, factual claims must remain traceable in the underlying sermon package.

The production system should preserve source attribution even when the spoken manuscript uses natural language.

---

## 20. Current Events

Current events should serve the sermon rather than hijack it.

Use current events when they:

* illuminate the sermon topic
* create useful cultural context
* demonstrate a relevant human tension
* help the congregation understand why the subject matters now

Avoid using news simply because it is recent.

Avoid sensationalism.

Avoid partisan framing unless the sermon specifically addresses the subject and the theological and pastoral context warrants it.

The sermon should remain meaningful after the news cycle ends.

---

## 21. Cultural Awareness

The sermon should understand the world the congregation actually inhabits.

Relevant cultural references may include:

* technology
* social media
* work
* family
* entertainment
* education
* politics
* economics
* cultural trends
* loneliness
* community
* generational differences
* artificial intelligence
* changing social norms

Cultural awareness does not mean cultural conformity.

The sermon should engage culture thoughtfully while allowing Scripture and theology to provide the governing framework.

---

## 22. Pastoral Voice

The default pastoral voice should be:

* sincere
* grounded
* compassionate
* confident without arrogance
* intellectually honest
* conversational
* hopeful without being simplistic

The preacher should sound like someone who is also participating in the human struggle being discussed.

Avoid unnecessary distance between preacher and congregation.

Prefer:

> "We"

when the preacher genuinely shares the human condition being discussed.

Avoid using:

> "You people..."

or language that unnecessarily places the congregation beneath the speaker.

---

## 23. Conviction Without Arrogance

A sermon can make strong claims without pretending every question is settled.

Confidence should correspond to certainty.

When Scripture is clear, speak clearly.

When interpretation is debated, qualify appropriately.

When the issue is theological synthesis, distinguish synthesis from explicit biblical teaching.

When the issue is uncertain, acknowledge uncertainty.

The sermon should never manufacture certainty for rhetorical power.

---

## 24. Gospel and Hope

When appropriate to the sermon and theological profile, the sermon should move beyond diagnosis toward hope.

Christian preaching should not merely identify:

* sin
* brokenness
* cultural problems
* personal failure
* human weakness

It should consider what God has done, is doing, or calls people toward in response.

Hope should not mean forced optimism.

Hope should arise from the theological substance of the sermon.

---

## 25. The Conclusion

The conclusion should feel like the sermon has arrived somewhere.

It should not simply stop because the manuscript has reached its target word count.

A strong conclusion may:

* return to the opening
* restate the big idea
* answer the central question
* summarize the movement
* offer a concrete response
* create reflection
* point toward hope
* invite prayer
* leave the congregation with a memorable final thought

The conclusion should generally become simpler rather than introduce several new ideas.

Avoid introducing an entirely new theological argument in the final minute.

---

## 26. Call to Response

A call to response should follow naturally from the sermon.

Possible responses include:

* believe
* trust
* repent
* forgive
* reconcile
* pray
* act
* wait
* serve
* change perspective
* seek help
* practice a discipline
* discuss the issue with someone
* respond to God's invitation

Not every sermon requires a dramatic altar-call-style ending.

The appropriate response depends on the sermon.

---

## 27. The Final Sentence

The final sentence should matter.

Whenever appropriate, the sermon should end with a concise expression of its central truth, invitation, or hope.

The final sentence should not be:

* a generic inspirational slogan
* a new idea
* an unnecessary disclaimer
* a summary of the production process
* an AI-sounding conclusion

The congregation should know what they are leaving with.

---

## 28. Avoiding AI-Sounding Sermons

The system should actively detect and remove language patterns commonly associated with generic AI-generated inspirational writing.

Avoid excessive phrases such as:

* "In a world that..."
* "Let that sink in."
* "Here's the thing..."
* "You need to understand..."
* "God is reminding you today..."
* "This changes everything."
* "And that's the beauty of..."
* "We need to lean into..."
* "At the end of the day..."
* "The truth is..."
* "Maybe you're here today because..."
* "God wants you to know..."

These phrases are not forbidden.

They become a problem when they appear because the system is relying on sermon clichés instead of communicating a specific thought.

Specificity should replace cliché.

---

## 29. Avoiding Manufactured Depth

A sermon should not confuse vagueness with profundity.

Statements such as:

> "Sometimes the thing we need most is the thing we least expect."

may sound profound while communicating almost nothing.

Prefer specific claims:

> "We often ask God to remove the uncertainty when Scripture instead teaches us how to remain faithful inside it."

The sermon should say something.

---

## 30. Avoiding Manufactured Emotion

The system should not artificially escalate emotional language toward the conclusion.

Do not assume that:

* louder = more powerful
* sadder = deeper
* more repetition = more meaningful
* more personal language = more authentic
* more dramatic language = more spiritual

Emotional weight should emerge from the actual content.

---

## 31. The Congregation Is Not an Audience of Abstractions

Write for actual people.

The sermon should account for the fact that a congregation may contain people who are:

* convinced
* skeptical
* grieving
* joyful
* exhausted
* angry
* curious
* new to Christianity
* longtime Christians
* knowledgeable about Scripture
* unfamiliar with Scripture
* struggling privately
* experiencing circumstances unknown to the preacher

Do not assume everyone begins from the same place.

When appropriate, acknowledge multiple perspectives without diluting the sermon.

---

## 32. Accessibility

The sermon should be understandable without requiring specialized theological knowledge.

When technical theological terms are necessary:

1. use the term accurately
2. explain it plainly
3. connect it to the sermon's purpose

Do not use theological vocabulary as a substitute for explanation.

---

## 33. Intellectual Substance

Accessible does not mean shallow.

A good sermon can be:

* intellectually serious
* theologically careful
* emotionally compelling
* practically useful
* conversational

These qualities are not opposites.

The goal is not to simplify the truth until nothing difficult remains.

The goal is to make difficult truth understandable.

---

## 34. Tension and Complexity

Do not resolve every tension artificially.

Some biblical and theological questions remain difficult.

When appropriate, the sermon may acknowledge:

> "I don't think this passage gives us an easy answer."

The existence of uncertainty does not make a sermon weak.

Honest complexity can build trust when handled clearly and pastorally.

---

## 35. Sermon Density

Not every sentence needs to carry maximum information.

Spoken communication requires breathing room.

Allow space for:

* reflection
* emotional processing
* humor
* silence
* repetition
* transitions
* audience comprehension

A sermon should not feel like the preacher is trying to deliver a textbook at speaking speed.

---

## 36. The Difference Between Teaching and Preaching

Teaching primarily seeks understanding.

Preaching seeks understanding that leads toward response.

A sermon should therefore contain teaching, but should not end with information.

The system should continually ask:

> So what does this mean for the people hearing it?

And then:

> What might they actually do, believe, reconsider, or become because of it?

---

## 37. Sermon Integrity

The sermon should preserve the distinction between:

* what Scripture says
* what the preacher believes
* what scholarship suggests
* what research demonstrates
* what an illustration depicts
* what the sermon proposes as application

These distinctions may not always be spoken explicitly to the congregation, but they must remain clear in the underlying sermon architecture.

Rhetorical confidence must never conceal evidentiary uncertainty.

---

## 38. Series Style

For a sermon series, individual sermons should feel related without becoming repetitive.

The series should establish:

* an overarching question or theme
* a progression
* distinct weekly central ideas
* recurring language or motifs where useful
* cumulative development

Each week should accomplish something the other weeks do not.

Do not divide one sermon into four pieces simply to create a series.

The audience should feel:

> "This week moved us somewhere."

---

## 39. Standalone Sermons

A standalone sermon should be complete on its own.

It should not assume the congregation heard a previous sermon unless the user explicitly specifies that context.

The sermon should establish its own:

* context
* central question
* biblical foundation
* argument
* application
* conclusion

---

## 40. Preacher Authenticity

The final sermon should be adaptable to the individual preacher.

The system should not attempt to imitate a specific living preacher unless explicitly authorized and appropriate.

Instead, it should provide material that can be shaped by the preacher's:

* personality
* theological convictions
* speaking style
* congregation
* experiences
* sense of humor
* pastoral relationship with the congregation

The sermon is a tool for the preacher.

It is not a replacement for the preacher's voice.

---

## 41. Human Revision

The generated sermon should be easy for a preacher to revise.

The preacher should be able to:

* remove sections
* rewrite language
* replace illustrations
* change applications
* alter theological emphasis
* add personal stories
* adjust tone
* reorder movements
* reject the proposed conclusion
* regenerate individual sections

The system should therefore favor modular sermon architecture over a monolithic block of prose.

---

## 42. Style Audit

Before a sermon is considered complete, evaluate:

### Clarity

* Is the central idea obvious?
* Can the argument be followed when heard aloud?
* Are important terms understandable?

### Spoken Quality

* Does the manuscript sound natural when spoken?
* Are sentences reasonably conversational?
* Is there sufficient rhythm and breathing room?

### Structure

* Does each movement advance the sermon?
* Is there unnecessary repetition?
* Does the conclusion resolve the sermon?

### Relevance

* Does the sermon connect with actual human experience?
* Are cultural references useful rather than decorative?

### Emotional Integrity

* Does the emotional movement arise naturally?
* Is there manipulation or manufactured intensity?

### Biblical Integrity

* Does the rhetorical treatment remain faithful to Scripture?
* Has rhetorical force distorted interpretation?

### Intellectual Integrity

* Are factual claims supported?
* Are uncertain claims appropriately qualified?
* Does the sermon avoid manufactured profundity?

### Authenticity

* Could a real pastor plausibly preach this?
* Does it sound like a human communicating with people rather than an AI generating inspirational prose?

---

## 43. Final Sermon Standard

The final sermon should satisfy the following test:

> **Is this something a thoughtful pastor could actually preach to real people, with confidence that it is faithful to Scripture, intellectually honest, pastorally responsible, emotionally genuine, and worth the congregation's time?**

If the answer is no, the sermon is not finished.

The goal is not to produce the most impressive sermon.

The goal is to produce a sermon that is:

**faithful enough to trust, clear enough to understand, human enough to hear, meaningful enough to remember, and practical enough to live.**
