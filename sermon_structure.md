# Sermon Structure

## 1. Purpose

A sermon is not merely an organized collection of biblical observations.

A sermon takes a biblical truth and leads people toward understanding, conviction, faith, obedience, worship, repentance, hope, or some other appropriate response.

The structure of a sermon therefore exists to serve the **message**, not the other way around.

The agent must not force every sermon into a predetermined outline. It should determine the most faithful and effective structure based on:

* The biblical text
* The central theological claim
* The intended audience
* The sermon's purpose
* The natural movement of the passage
* The appropriate response to the truth being proclaimed

Structure should make the message easier to follow, remember, and respond to without artificially imposing organization that is absent from the text or argument.

---

# 2. The Central Principle

Every sermon should have a discernible **central idea**.

The sermon should be able to answer:

> What is this sermon ultimately trying to say?

The central idea should be:

* Grounded in Scripture
* Theologically faithful
* Specific enough to guide the sermon
* Broad enough to encompass the major movements of the sermon
* Relevant to the intended audience
* Capable of producing an appropriate response

Every major structural movement should contribute to this central idea.

If a section does not materially advance the sermon, the agent should question whether it belongs.

---

# 3. Text Determines Structure

The agent should begin with the biblical text rather than a preferred sermon template.

The structure may arise from:

* The natural structure of the passage
* The development of an argument
* A theological tension
* A question raised by the text
* A movement from problem to resolution
* A movement from observation to interpretation to response
* A repeated biblical theme
* A narrative progression
* A contrast presented by the author
* A promise and its fulfillment
* A command and its implications
* A doctrine and its consequences
* A biblical character's experience
* A larger canonical or redemptive-historical movement

The agent should preserve meaningful distinctions within the text rather than flattening them into artificial sermon points.

Likewise, it should not assume that every observable textual division deserves to become a sermon point.

**Biblical structure and sermon structure are related, but they are not identical.**

---

# 4. Sermon Movement

A sermon should have meaningful movement.

The listener should be able to perceive that the sermon is going somewhere.

Movement may take different forms:

```text
Question → Answer
Problem → Resolution
Need → Provision
Brokenness → Hope
Observation → Meaning → Response
Promise → Fulfillment
Command → Motivation → Practice
Truth → Implication → Response
Conflict → Revelation → Transformation
Death → Life
Fear → Faith
Ignorance → Understanding
Sin → Repentance → Grace
```

These are examples, not required formulas.

The agent should choose movement that naturally serves the message.

A sermon that contains several true points but never develops toward a meaningful conclusion is structurally weak.

---

# 5. Introduction

The introduction should establish enough orientation for the listener to understand why the sermon matters.

An introduction may:

* Raise a meaningful question
* Introduce a tension
* Identify a recognizable human experience
* Present a problem
* Establish the biblical setting
* Begin with the text itself
* Tell a brief story
* Present a surprising observation
* Establish the stakes of the subject
* Connect the biblical world to the listener's world

The introduction should not attempt to explain the entire sermon.

Its primary function is to create **orientation and attention** and establish the path into the biblical text.

The introduction should move toward Scripture rather than becoming an unrelated motivational speech.

### Introduction test

By the time the sermon enters its main body, the listener should understand:

* What we are talking about
* Why it matters
* Where we are going

---

# 6. Establishing the Text

The sermon should give the congregation sufficient context to understand the passage being preached.

The amount of context should be proportional to the passage and audience.

Relevant context may include:

* Who wrote the passage
* Who received it
* Historical circumstances
* Literary context
* The surrounding argument
* Important cultural background
* Key terms
* The position of the passage within the biblical book
* Connections to the larger biblical story

Context exists to illuminate the text.

It should not become a lecture that delays the sermon unnecessarily.

The agent should distinguish between:

**Context necessary for understanding**

and

**Interesting information that does not materially affect interpretation.**

Only the former normally belongs in the sermon.

---

# 7. Exposition

The sermon should faithfully explain what the biblical text communicates.

Exposition may involve:

1. Observation — What does the text say?
2. Interpretation — What does the text mean?
3. Significance — Why does this matter?
4. Connection — How does this relate to the larger biblical and theological witness?
5. Response — What should we do with this truth?

These functions may appear in different orders depending on the passage.

Exposition should not be confused with explaining every word.

The goal is not maximum information.

The goal is **faithful understanding of the passage's meaning and significance**.

---

# 8. Major Sermon Movements

Major movements should represent meaningful developments in the sermon rather than arbitrary divisions.

A major movement should generally accomplish at least one of the following:

* Advance the central argument
* Reveal an important truth
* Resolve a meaningful tension
* Develop the theological claim
* Deepen understanding of the text
* Expose a human condition
* Reveal God's character or action
* Establish the basis for application
* Move the listener toward the intended response

Major movements should be distinguishable without becoming mechanically repetitive.

For example, a sermon should not manufacture:

> Point 1
> Point 2
> Point 3
> Point 4

simply because four observations were discovered.

The structure should follow the **logic of the sermon**, not the number of observations available.

---

# 9. Application

Application is not an optional motivational section added after the "real sermon."

Application demonstrates the implications of biblical truth for life.

Application may address:

* Belief
* Worship
* Repentance
* Obedience
* Relationships
* Character
* Decision-making
* Suffering
* Hope
* Fear
* Work
* Family
* Community
* Mission
* Spiritual formation
* Perseverance

Application should arise naturally from the biblical meaning of the text.

The agent must avoid generic application that could be attached to almost any sermon.

Weak:

> We should trust God more.

Stronger:

> Because God remains faithful when circumstances obscure His promises, believers can continue obeying Him without requiring immediate evidence that obedience will produce the outcome they want.

Application should answer:

> If this is true, what difference should it make?

---

# 10. Application Must Respect the Text

The agent must not manufacture applications that Scripture does not reasonably support.

Application should preserve the distinction between:

* What the text explicitly commands
* What the text clearly implies
* What is a reasonable theological implication
* What is a pastoral suggestion
* What is merely the preacher's personal preference

The farther an application moves from the explicit meaning of the passage, the more carefully it should be framed.

The sermon should never use Scripture as a pretext for unrelated advice.

---

# 11. Gospel and Redemptive Movement

When appropriate to the text and theological framework, sermons should locate the passage within the larger biblical story.

The agent should consider how the passage relates to:

* Creation
* Fall
* God's covenant purposes
* Israel
* Promise
* Christ
* Cross
* Resurrection
* Church
* Kingdom
* New creation

This does not mean every sermon should artificially force an explicit Christological conclusion into the final thirty seconds.

Instead, the agent should ask:

> Where does this text belong within the whole story of Scripture?

The answer should emerge from legitimate biblical and theological connections.

---

# 12. Christ-Centeredness

For Christian preaching, Christ should not be treated merely as a mandatory closing reference.

When the text legitimately points toward Christ, the sermon should explain that connection.

Christ-centered preaching may occur through:

* Direct reference to Christ
* Fulfillment of Old Testament expectation
* Christ's teaching
* Christ's work
* Christ's character
* The gospel
* The new covenant
* The cross
* The resurrection
* Union with Christ
* The kingdom inaugurated through Christ
* The hope of Christ's return

The agent must avoid forced connections that the text does not support.

The goal is not:

> Mention Jesus.

The goal is:

> Faithfully understand this text within the biblical revelation that culminates in Christ.

---

# 13. Emotional and Spiritual Movement

A sermon communicates not only information but significance.

The structure should account for the emotional and spiritual weight of the message.

Depending on the text, a sermon may move the listener through:

* Curiosity
* Recognition
* Conviction
* Grief
* Wonder
* Gratitude
* Fear
* Hope
* Comfort
* Courage
* Repentance
* Joy
* Worship
* Resolve

The agent should never manufacture emotion merely for rhetorical effect.

Emotional movement should emerge from the truth being proclaimed.

The sermon should not manipulate the listener into feeling something that the message itself does not warrant.

---

# 14. Transitions

Transitions should make the movement of the sermon understandable.

A transition may:

* Summarize what has just been established
* Explain why the next section matters
* Answer a question raised by the previous section
* Introduce a new tension
* Connect two related ideas
* Shift from explanation to application
* Move from the text to the larger biblical story

Transitions should generally be concise.

The sermon should not repeatedly announce its structure with phrases such as:

> "Now point number two."

when a more natural transition would communicate the movement.

---

# 15. Conclusion

The conclusion should bring the sermon to its intended destination.

It should not merely stop when the final point is finished.

A strong conclusion may:

* Restate the central truth
* Resolve the opening tension
* Summarize the theological claim
* Call for an appropriate response
* Return to an opening image or question
* Reinforce hope
* Point toward Christ
* Lead into worship
* Leave the listener with a memorable truth

The conclusion should be proportionate to the sermon.

It should not introduce an entirely new theological argument.

### Conclusion test

The listener should be able to answer:

> What was the sermon saying, and what should I do with it?

---

# 16. The Sermon's Landing Point

Every sermon should have a deliberate **landing point**.

The landing point is the final truth, realization, invitation, warning, hope, or response toward which the sermon has been moving.

The agent should identify this before finalizing the structure.

Examples:

* A truth the congregation should believe
* A sin the congregation should repent of
* A promise the congregation should trust
* A person or attribute of God the congregation should worship
* A command the congregation should obey
* A hope the congregation should embrace
* A way of seeing their circumstances differently
* A gospel reality the congregation should rest in

The landing point should be earned by the sermon.

It should not appear suddenly in the conclusion.

---

# 17. Narrative Sermons

Narrative passages require special structural consideration.

The agent should preserve the narrative's:

* Characters
* Conflict
* Setting
* Development
* Turning points
* Revelation
* Resolution
* Theological significance

The sermon should not necessarily reduce a narrative into abstract propositions.

Narrative can communicate theology through what happens.

The agent should allow the story to carry appropriate weight while still explaining its theological significance.

---

# 18. Epistles and Argumentative Passages

For argumentative or doctrinal passages, structure should generally respect the author's reasoning.

The agent should identify:

* The claim
* Supporting arguments
* Assumptions
* Contrasts
* Conclusions
* Implications

The sermon should not treat a logical argument as merely a collection of inspirational statements.

Where the text develops an argument, the sermon should help the listener follow that argument.

---

# 19. Poetry and Wisdom Literature

Poetry should be structured with attention to:

* Imagery
* Parallelism
* Repetition
* Contrast
* Emotional progression
* Metaphor
* Wisdom principles
* Theological themes

The agent should avoid flattening poetic language into literal propositions when doing so destroys the intended meaning.

Likewise, poetic observations should not be treated as universal promises or commands without considering genre and context.

---

# 20. Doctrinal Sermons

A doctrinal sermon should still have movement.

It should not become merely:

> Definition → supporting verses → definition → supporting verses.

A doctrinal sermon may move through:

```text
Question → Biblical Revelation → Explanation → Implications → Response
```

or:

```text
Misunderstanding → Biblical Truth → Clarification → Consequences → Hope
```

Doctrine should ultimately connect to knowing God, understanding the gospel, living faithfully, worship, hope, or some other appropriate response.

---

# 21. The Agent Must Avoid Structural Formula

The following are not mandatory:

* Three points
* Five points
* Repeated alliteration
* Identical point lengths
* A story at the beginning
* A story at the end
* A joke
* A dramatic conclusion
* A specific number of illustrations
* A specific sermon length

These may be useful rhetorical tools, but they must never become requirements independent of the sermon.

The agent should prefer **organic structure over formulaic structure**.

---

# 22. Illustrations

Illustrations should clarify, embody, or illuminate the sermon's central truth.

Appropriate illustrations may include:

* Personal experience
* Historical events
* Literature
* Everyday life
* Cultural observations
* Hypothetical situations
* Analogies
* Stories

Illustrations should serve the sermon.

The agent should not add an illustration simply because a section feels "dry."

An illustration should be evaluated by asking:

> Does this help the listener understand, feel, remember, or apply the truth?

If not, it should probably be removed.

---

# 23. Repetition

Strategic repetition is encouraged when it improves comprehension or retention.

Important ideas may be repeated through:

* Restatement
* Variation
* Summary
* Imagery
* Application
* Conclusion

However, repetition should develop understanding rather than merely consume time.

The agent should distinguish between:

**Reinforcement**

and

**Redundancy.**

---

# 24. Sermon Length

Sermon structure should be proportional to the intended length.

A longer sermon does not require more points.

Additional time should generally be used to:

* Develop important ideas
* Explain difficult passages
* Establish necessary context
* Explore meaningful implications
* Allow theological ideas to breathe
* Develop appropriate application

The agent should not expand a sermon merely to meet a target word count.

Likewise, a short sermon should not omit essential biblical reasoning simply to remain concise.

---

# 25. Structural Integrity Test

Before finalizing a sermon, the agent should evaluate:

### Central Idea

Can the sermon be summarized in one clear theological statement?

### Text

Does the structure arise naturally from the biblical text and its meaning?

### Movement

Does the sermon actually go somewhere?

### Coherence

Does each major movement contribute to the central idea?

### Exposition

Does the sermon explain what the text means rather than merely mentioning it?

### Application

Does the sermon demonstrate why the truth matters?

### Gospel

Where appropriate, does the sermon locate the text within the larger biblical story and its fulfillment?

### Emotional Integrity

Does the emotional movement arise naturally from the truth rather than manipulation?

### Conclusion

Does the sermon arrive at a deliberate landing point?

### Economy

Could anything be removed without weakening the sermon?

If removing a section does not materially weaken the sermon, the section should be considered unnecessary.

---

# 26. Structural Priority

When structural decisions conflict, prioritize them in this order:

1. **Faithfulness to Scripture**
2. **Theological coherence**
3. **Clarity of the central idea**
4. **Natural movement of the text and argument**
5. **Meaningful application**
6. **Pastoral and emotional resonance**
7. **Rhetorical effectiveness**
8. **Memorability**

Rhetorical effectiveness must never override biblical or theological faithfulness.

A memorable sermon that distorts Scripture is a failed sermon.

---

# 27. Core Rule

The agent should never ask:

> "What sermon template should I use?"

It should ask:

> "What does this text need this sermon to become?"

Structure exists to serve Scripture, communicate truth clearly, and lead people toward an appropriate response.

**The sermon should feel constructed because it is intentional, but it should not feel formulaic because the structure belongs to the message.**
